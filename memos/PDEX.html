Pro-Dex: the surgical screwdriver spec'd into everyone else's FDA filings

Thesis

Pro-Dex designs and builds powered surgical handpieces, mostly cranio-maxillofacial and orthopedic drivers, for major medical device companies. Its products sit inside those customers' FDA regulatory filings. Swapping suppliers means new filings, new validation, and program risk nobody in medtech wants. That creates deep switching costs and a sticky revenue base.

Nobody covers the stock. FY25 revenue grew 23.7 percent to $66.6M and net income jumped over 300 percent to $9.0M as new-product ramps hit scale. Growth continued into FY26: nine-month revenue ran $57.1M, up 16 percent, with net income of $10.8M ($3.27 diluted). The board, anchored by the Van Kirk family and value investors, buys back stock aggressively.

Our original screen called this 15x trailing earnings. Verification corrected that. At $62.74 the trailing P/E is 16.9x, and stripping a non-operating gain puts the operating multiple near 19-20x. The stock is less cheap than the first pass claimed. We still like it because the moat quality, growth rate, and total neglect justify the fuller multiple.

Business

Pro-Dex operates from Irvine, California as an OEM designer and contract manufacturer of powered surgical instruments. Its core products are battery-powered screwdrivers and drivers with embedded torque-limiting software, used in skull, facial, and orthopedic surgery. Customers are large medtech companies who put their own brands on the devices.

The business model matters more than the product list. Pro-Dex engineers the device, wins the design into a customer's regulatory submission, then manufactures it for the life of the program. FY25 revenue was $66.6M against $53.8M in FY24. Gross margin ran 29 percent in FY25, up from 27 percent, then slipped to 31 percent in the FY26 third quarter from 33 percent a year earlier on product mix. The current growth engine is the largest customer's next-generation orthopedic handpiece.

Why the market is wrong

No analyst covers Pro-Dex. The float is small, the family-influenced board does not court Wall Street, and the company sells unbranded components inside other people's products. Screens see a $200M industrial with lumpy history. They miss that earnings quadrupled in FY25 and kept growing in FY26.

The market also misprices the moat. This is a micro-cap that behaves like a spec'd-in aerospace supplier. Once a handpiece is written into a customer's FDA filing, the revenue annuity runs for years, and Pro-Dex earns follow-on repair and refurbishment work. PDEX itself traded above 25x trailing earnings during prior growth spurts. Today the operating multiple sits at 19-20x while medical device OEM suppliers and CDMOs trade at 20-30x. We pay a market multiple for an above-market franchise nobody watches.

Valuation

TTM EPS is $3.63, which puts the trailing P/E at 16.9x. That headline flatters. FY26 Q3 EPS of $1.20 included a $2.3M realized gain on Monogram Technologies CVRs, worth around $0.70 of TTM EPS. Strip that and the operating-earnings multiple lands near 19-20x. We anchor on the adjusted figure.

The math still works. Nine-month FY26 net income of $10.8M annualizes to roughly $14M, most of it operating. Hold the operating multiple flat and earnings growth alone drives the return. A re-rate toward the 25x the stock has carried before, or toward peer CDMOs at 20-30x, adds another 25 to 50 percent. The buyback shrinks the denominator the whole time. We do not underwrite the re-rate. We underwrite continued mid-teens growth at a fair price.

Risks

Customer concentration kills this thesis. The top customer has historically represented over half of revenue, and verification confirmed that the largest customer drove nearly all recent growth. A single insourcing decision or program loss would cut earnings sharply and probably halve the stock. Position size has to respect that.

Two verification caveats deserve honesty. First, our original valuation claim was stale: the true trailing multiple is 16.9x, not 15x, and the operating multiple is 19-20x once the $2.3M CVR gain comes out. The cheapness argument is softer than the screen suggested. Second, gross margin slipped to 31 percent from 33 percent on mix in the latest quarter. The stock also sits near the top of a 52-week range that started at $23.47, so entry timing carries momentum risk. Verification confidence on this idea was medium, lower than our hardest-asset names.

Catalysts

FY26 fourth-quarter and full-year results, due around September 2026 for the June 30 fiscal year end, should show whether the nine-month 16 percent growth pace held. Continued ramp of the largest customer's next-gen orthopedic handpiece drives near-term numbers. Any new program win from a second major customer would attack the concentration discount directly. Ongoing buybacks at a $200M cap retire meaningful float each quarter. Eventually, a first analyst initiation or index inclusion ends the neglect, and neglect ending is the whole trade.
